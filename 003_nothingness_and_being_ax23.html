Between Determination and Manifestation

A Philosophical Study of Ax 23: Nothingness and Being

Abstract

Ax 23

Ax 23 situates philosophical thought between two fundamental poles: being and nothingness. Nothingness is defined as the absence of all determination; being is defined through manifestation in the world; thought is presented as a field that includes both poles without reducing either to the other.

This structure does not establish two independent substances or two symmetrical objects. Instead, it articulates a tension between manifestation and the absence of determination, between what becomes fact and what cannot be constituted as fact. Ax 23.1 establishes opposition. Ax 23.2 gives being a vector toward factual manifestation. Ax 23.3 relocates the opposition within thought.

Nothingness cannot manifest as a fact without ceasing to be the absence of determination, yet the concept of nothingness remains indispensable to philosophical thought as the pole against which being, manifestation, difference, possibility, and limit become intelligible. Ax 23 consequently functions as an inquiry into the conditions under which anything can appear, be distinguished, and enter the world as a fact. It also prepares the movement toward Ax 24, where thought encounters the limit of saying, the excess of expression, silence, and direct experience.

I. The Axiomatic Structure of the Question

Ax 23 is entitled “Nothingness and Being.” Its three propositions form a progression. The first distinguishes nothingness from being through opposition. The second defines being by its movement toward factual manifestation. The third situates thought within the configuration constituted by these two poles.

Ax 23.1 — Nothingness is the absence of all determination. [ (■ “Nothing”) ⊖ (■ “Being”) ]
Ax 23.2 — Being is what manifests in the world. [ (■ “Being”) → (■ “Fact”) ]
Ax 23.3 — Nothingness and being are the poles of philosophical thought. [ (■ “Thought”) ⊑ ⟨ (■ “Being”), (■ “Nothing”) ⟩ ]

The sequence is decisive. Ax 23 does not begin by asking what particular beings are. It begins by distinguishing determination from its absence. Only after this distinction does it describe being as manifestation. Finally, it identifies philosophical thought as the field in which this distinction is sustained.

The question of being is therefore inseparable from the question of determination. To be is not presented as mere abstract presence. Being is what manifests in the world and becomes accessible as fact. Nothingness, by contrast, is not presented as a second object or a second kind of being. Ax 23.1 defines it as the absence of all determination, while Ax 23.2 alone gives being a vector toward fact. Ax 23 is thus not a static inventory of two entities. It is an architecture of asymmetrical tension.

II. Nothingness as the Absence of All Determination

The expression “all determination” must be given its full force. Nothingness is not the absence of one particular object, one property, one event, or one fact. The disappearance of a particular fact leaves other facts intact. The negation of one determination remains dependent on a field of other determinations. Nothingness, however, is defined as the absence of determination as such.

A determination makes something distinguishable. It gives form, relation, position, limit, or identity. To determine something is to allow it to emerge from indifference as something identifiable. Determination is therefore connected to differentiation.

Ax 2.1 — We do not perceive totality, but distinct fragments. [ ⊙ → ▲ ]

The emergence of a distinct fragment requires differentiation. Something appears because it is not everything else. It occupies a position within a network of relations. It can be identified only because a distinction has been produced.

Ax 23.1 does not authorize a temporal “before” or a spatial “beyond” of determination, because either would already assign a relation or position. It marks the conceptual limit at which no distinction can be maintained. A property, position, number, or identity cannot be attributed to nothingness without introducing the very determination the axiom excludes.

Nothingness must therefore not be confused with an object described as empty. An empty vessel, a blank field, or the empty set remains determinate: each has an identity, formal properties, and relations, while “empty” specifies the absence of some content or members. In every such case, something is preserved to which emptiness is attributed. Ax 23.1 is more radical: it does not name a state in which contents have been removed while an object remains; it names the absence of the determinations by which object, content, boundary, and relation could be distinguished. Nor is nothingness merely an unknown fact. An unknown fact remains situated within the field of inquiry.

Ax 11.1 — To know is to reduce uncertainty in facts. [ (● “Unknown”) → (■ “Inquiry”) → (● “Known”) ]

An unknown fact can become known because inquiry can further determine it. The term “nothingness” does not function in the same sequence. Once thought attributes positive properties to what Ax 23.1 defines as the absence of all determination, it is no longer describing nothingness but constructing a determinate concept or representation. The first tension of Ax 23 is therefore the tension between nothingness as defined and the necessary determination involved in referring to it.

III. Opposition Without Symmetry

The operator ⊖ expresses negation, contrast, or dialectical tension. It does not require both terms to possess the same structure. Nothingness and being are opposed, but they are not simply two equivalent objects positioned on opposite sides of a neutral boundary.

Ax 23.2 directs being toward fact. Ax 23.1 gives nothingness no corresponding vector: what is defined as the absence of all determination cannot become a fact without ceasing to satisfy that definition. The opposition is therefore asymmetrical.

Ax 23.2 — Being is what manifests in the world. [ (■ “Being”) → (■ “Fact”) ]

Its symbolic relation to being is one of contrast rather than a parallel movement toward fact. This difference prevents nothingness from being treated as a second kind of being.

If nothingness were to manifest in the world in the same manner as being, it would become a fact. Yet every fact is determinate enough to enter a relational configuration.

Ax 1.1 — The world unfolds as a relational set of all facts. [ ⟨ ●₁ ⊡ ●₂ ⊡ ●₃ ⊡ ●ₙ ⟩ → ⊙ ]
Ax 1.2 — What exists is not isolated, but always in relation to something else. [ ● ─ ⊑ ─ ● ]

A fact belongs to the world by entering relations with other facts. Nothingness, understood as the absence of all determination, cannot enter such a relation as a fact without receiving a relational determination. When “Nothing” is positioned, contrasted, named, or included, what becomes determinate is the sign or concept within thought—not nothingness as the absence of all determination.

This distinction clarifies rather than invalidates the symbolic formula. In [ (■ “Nothing”) ⊖ (■ “Being”) ], ■ gives concrete symbolic form to the term “Nothing” inside the diagram; it need not be read as an assertion that nothingness itself appears as an observable fact. The notation manifests the concept required for the opposition, while Ax 23.1 preserves the content of that concept as the absence of all determination. The tension belongs to the act of representation: thought must determine a sign in order to orient itself toward what cannot be positively determined.

IV. Being as Manifestation

Ax 23.2 does not define being as a hidden substance behind appearances. It defines being by manifestation. The vector from being to fact presents factual manifestation as the mode in which being becomes accessible within the world.

This proposition connects Ax 23 to the opening architecture of totality. Ax 1.1 describes the world as a relational set of facts, while Ax 1.3 states:

Ax 1.3 — Every fact is a possible configuration of reality. [ ● → ⟨ ●₁, ●₂, ●₃ ⟩ ]

Being is consequently inseparable from configuration. What manifests does not appear in absolute isolation. It takes form within a field of relations. A fact is not merely present; it is positioned, connected, distinguished, and capable of affecting other facts.

Manifestation therefore includes more than visibility. Something may manifest through an action, a consequence, a structure, a memory, a sign, or a transformation. To manifest is to acquire factual presence within the relational world.

Ax 6.1 — Experience is not mere representation — it modifies reality. [ (■ “Self”) → (● → ●’) ]
Ax 6.3 — Every transformation leaves a trace in the world. [ (● → ●’) ⊑ (■ “Memory”) ]

Manifested being is involved in processes of transformation. Facts enter relations, actions change configurations, and consequences leave traces. Being is therefore not exhausted by static presence. It manifests in a world that is continuously reconfigured.

The movement from being to fact also establishes a criterion of philosophical discipline. Assertions about being must remain connected to manifestation. When thought separates being completely from every possible fact, it risks transforming being into an empty word without relational function. Ax 23.2 resists this abstraction. Being matters because it manifests.

V. Fact, Determination, and Relation

The transition from being to fact explains why determination is essential. A fact must be sufficiently determinate to be distinguishable from other facts. It must possess a position within a configuration.

Ax 4.1 — Every experience is an arrangement of facts. [ (●₁ ⊑ ●₂ ⊑ ●₃) → ■ (“Mental Schema”) ]

Experience does not receive undifferentiated being. It encounters arrangements. Facts are ordered, connected, remembered, interpreted, and transformed. The subject encounters being through determinate configurations.

Ax 5.1 — Not everything that appears as a fact is real. [ (● “Appearance”) ⊖ (● “Reality”) ]

Manifestation and interpretation can diverge. Appearance can be mistaken for reality, and thought can establish false connections. The fact that being manifests does not mean that every perceived manifestation has been correctly understood.

Ax 16.2 — Falsehood is an erroneous configuration of facts. [ (■ “Error”) → (● “Deceptive Representation”) ]
Ax 5.3 — Illusion is a false connection between facts. [ (●₁ ⊑ # ⊑ ●₂) ]

Falsehood is not nothingness. Illusion is not nothingness. Error, illusion, contradiction, and the unknown remain within determinate structures. An error is something that can be identified as error. An illusion connects facts falsely. A contradiction places determinate propositions in tension. The unknown marks the edge of knowledge.

Nothingness, by contrast, is the absence of all determination. It is more radical than negating the truth of a proposition. It is not the claim that a particular fact does not exist. It is the conceptual removal of the very conditions through which facts become identifiable.

VI. Thought Between the Two Poles

Ax 23.3 situates thought within the configuration formed by being and nothingness. Thought does not stand outside this polarity as an entirely neutral observer.

Ax 23.3 — Nothingness and being are the poles of philosophical thought. [ (■ “Thought”) ⊑ ⟨ (■ “Being”), (■ “Nothing”) ⟩ ]

Thought is related both to what manifests and to the limit implied by the absence of all determination. It thinks facts, absences among facts, unrealized possibilities, and unknowns; Ax 23 requires it not to identify any of these determinate modes with nothingness.

The operator ⊑ expresses inclusion or derivation without eliminating relative autonomy. Thought is included within the field configured by the two poles. The use of ⟨ ⟩ indicates a set or configuration. Being and nothingness establish the conceptual field within which philosophical thought operates.

The pole of being orients inquiry toward manifestation, fact, relation, and world. The pole of nothingness orients it toward the limit of determination. These are functions of the polarity within thought, not actions performed by nothingness. Philosophical thought moves between the poles because neither can simply eliminate the other.

If thought attends only to being, it may treat every form of absence as merely another fact. If it attends only to nothingness, it loses the determinations required for thought to articulate anything at all. The poles generate tension, and that tension is productive because it prevents thought from becoming fixed at either extreme.

VII. The Paradox of Thinking Nothingness

To form the thought of nothingness is already to perform an act. The act has form, duration, language, and conceptual orientation. It manifests within consciousness and can be expressed through signs.

Ax 3.1 — We describe facts through signs. [ ● → “Signs” ]

When thought names “Nothing,” it introduces a sign. The sign becomes part of a linguistic configuration. It can be written, spoken, contrasted with another term, and placed within a symbolic diagram. What acquires linguistic determination is the sign and its conceptual role, not the absence of all determination itself.

This produces a paradox, but not necessarily an error. The paradox reveals the boundary between nothingness and the thought of nothingness. Nothingness is the absence of all determination; the thought of nothingness is a determinate act oriented toward that absence.

Ax 3.2 — Language traces a diagram of reality. [ “Word” ─ ⊑ ─ (■ “Meaning”) ]

A diagram is not identical to what it diagrams. Language can orient thought toward nothingness, but it cannot convert nothingness into an ordinary linguistic object without changing its conceptual status. This is why Ax 23 must be traversed rather than merely defined. Every definition of nothingness risks contradicting the content of the definition.

Yet refusing to think nothingness would conceal the limit that makes determination intelligible. Thought must therefore sustain a disciplined tension: it gives determinate form to a concept whose content is the absence of all determination.

VIII. Nothingness and the Limit of Language

Ax 23 is closely connected to the architecture of linguistic limit. When language reaches a limit, thought does not necessarily terminate. The limit can become the point from which language reflects on its own operations.

Ax 3.3 — The limit of a language dislocates into the beginning of its meta-language. [ (■ “Language”) → # → (■ “Meta-Language”) ]

The failure to express nothingness directly becomes an occasion to examine how determination, naming, and representation function. The concept of nothingness operates not as an object beyond language but as pressure upon language. Every attempt to speak it exposes the conditions and limits of speech.

Ax 11.3 — The unknown is what lies beyond language. [ (■ “Edge of Knowledge”) → # ]

Nothingness and the unknown must remain distinct. The unknown may suggest that something lies beyond current knowledge or expression. Nothingness is not simply a hidden region awaiting discovery. A hidden region would still contain possible determinations.

Ax 15.1 — Every system has a limit beyond which it cannot proceed. [ (■ “System”) → (■ “Boundary”) → # ]

Ax 23.1 marks such a limit for the system of determination. Thought can move toward the absence of all determination, but it cannot establish that absence as a positive fact within the system without contradiction.

Ax 15.3 — The limit is not negation but the condition of the possible. [ (■ “Horizon”) → ⟨ ● (Reality), # (Beyond) ⟩ ]

The inability to determine nothingness positively protects the distinction between nothingness and being. The limit allows thought to recognize that not everything can be converted into a fact without remainder. Within this reading, the concept of nothingness functions as a condition for thinking the possible limits of manifestation.

IX. Nothingness Is Not the Absurd

Ax 17.2 — The absurd is the absence of meaningful connection. [ (■ “Logical Divergence”) → (■ “Absurd”) ]

The absurd concerns connection. It arises where meaningful relation fails. Nothingness concerns determination itself. An absurd configuration may still contain determinate elements. The elements are present, but their connection lacks intelligible meaning.

No element can be attributed to nothingness, because any element would already introduce determination. Similarly, nonsense is not nothingness. Nonsense manifests as language, sound, gesture, or configuration. It can be experienced as nonsense only within a contrast with sense.

Ax 17.3 — Existence itself is a play between sense and nonsense. [ (■ “World”) ⊑ (⟨ ● (Sense), ● (Nonsense) ⟩) ]

This play remains within existence. Both sense and nonsense occur as determinate possibilities. Nothingness cannot be placed as one move within that play, because doing so would give it a determinate position. Ax 23.1 instead marks the absence of the determinations required for moves, distinctions, and relations to be identified.

Confusing nothingness with absurdity would reduce it to a problem of interpretation. Confusing it with nonsense would reduce it to a problem of language. Ax 23.1 makes a more radical claim: nothingness is the absence of all determination, not merely the absence of meaning.

X. Nothingness, Possibility, and Contingency

Ax 22.3 — The world is a configuration among infinite alternatives. [ (■ “Reality”) ⊑ ⟨ (■ “Possible 1”), (■ “Possible 2”), (■ “Possible N”) ⟩ ]

The actual world is one configuration among possibilities. A possibility is not nothingness. It possesses a possible determination, even when it has not become actual. It belongs to a field of alternatives.

Ax 13.3 — Necessity and possibility define the space of reality. [ (■ “Determinism”) ⊑ (■ “Freedom”) → (■ “World”) ]

Possibility remains connected to determination because a possibility is always the possibility of some configuration. Nothingness cannot be identified with the unactualized. The unactualized may still be represented as a determinate alternative.

Ax 13.2 — Contingency is the absence of deterministic constraint. [ (■ “Contingency”) ⊑ (● “Unpredictable Event”) ]

The absence of deterministic constraint does not imply the absence of all determination. A contingent event is still an event. It manifests as a fact even though it was not necessitated absolutely.

Ax 22.1 — Nothing is necessary in an absolute sense. [ (■ “Possibility”) ⊑ (■ “Contingency”) ]

Here “Nothing” functions grammatically within a proposition about necessity; it should not be confused with the defined nothingness of Ax 23.1. The proposition denies absolute necessity. It does not assert that nothingness is necessary or that reality is identical with nothingness.

The world remains a configuration. Being manifests through one configuration among alternatives. Nothingness is not an alternative configuration beside these possibilities, because an alternative configuration is already determinate. The possibility that something might not occur is not identical to nothingness. Non-occurrence remains meaningful only within a field in which other events do occur.

XI. The Generative Function of Thinking Nothingness

Although nothingness is the absence of all determination, the thought of nothingness has a generative function. It exposes the contingency of every particular determination.

A fact is this fact rather than another. A configuration is actual rather than merely possible. A form has emerged where another form might have emerged. The contrast with nothingness removes the temptation to treat any given determination as absolute.

Ax 22.2 — Necessity is merely a relation between conditions. [ (■ “If A, then B”) → (■ “Local Determinism”) ]

Determinate reality is relational and conditional. The concept of nothingness stands at the conceptual edge of these conditions without providing another condition. The contrast it establishes prevents conditional necessity from being mistaken for absolute necessity.

The thought of nothingness thus destabilizes the claim that what is must have been exactly as it is. Being manifests, but its manifested configuration is not automatically absolute.

This does not make nothingness a causal force. Nothingness does not act, choose, or produce. To describe it as an active agent would determine it. Its generative function belongs to thought: by confronting the absence of all determination, thought becomes capable of seeing determinate reality as determinate rather than inevitable.

XII. Being as Exposure to Transformation

Because being manifests as fact, it becomes exposed to relation and transformation. What enters the world can affect and be affected.

Ax 6.2 — Action is the concrete manifestation of will. [ (■ “Will”) → (● “Choice”) → (■ “Action”) ]

Action demonstrates a specific form of the movement from being to fact. Will becomes choice, and choice becomes concrete action. Once manifested, action produces consequences that enter totality.

Ax 7.4 — The value of an action is shown in its effects on totality. [ (■ “Action”) ⊑ (■ “World”) → (■ “Responsibility”) ]

Being is therefore not only a theoretical category. Manifested being participates in ethical consequence. To manifest is to enter a field where relations matter.

Ax 23 does not present nothingness as an agent or fact from which ethical consequences follow. The thought of nothingness, however, can change the subject’s relation to manifestation. It can intensify awareness that actions are not guaranteed by absolute necessity and that every determinate act excludes other possibilities.

The ethical significance of Ax 23 lies not in deriving values directly from nothingness but in clarifying the fragility and contingency of manifested forms. Actions matter because they become facts. They leave traces. They transform relational totality. Being enters responsibility through manifestation.

XIII. The Subject as a Manifestation That Thinks the Limit of Its Own Determination

Ax 2.2 — The subject emerges in the distinction between self and world. [ ▲ → (■ “Self”) ⊑ (■ “World”) ]

The subject is not outside being. It is a determinate manifestation within the world. Yet the subject can form and traverse the thought of nothingness. This creates a recursive structure: a manifestation of being gives conceptual form to the absence of all determination.

Thought does not thereby become nothingness. It remains a determinate process. It can, however, orient itself toward the limit at which it can no longer represent the absence of thought, subject, world, and distinction without reintroducing determination.

This is not equivalent to imagining an empty space. Space is already a determination. Nor is it equivalent to imagining darkness, silence, or solitude. Each is an experience with structure.

The thought of nothingness therefore confronts its own inability to represent what it seeks. Every image gives form. Every concept distinguishes. Every sentence establishes relations.

The subject discovers that nothingness cannot be experienced as an ordinary object of consciousness. Any experience is already an arrangement of facts, as stated in Ax 4.1. The experience of “nothing” is therefore always the experience of some determinate absence: the absence of sound, objects, movement, memory, or expected presence.

Nothingness remains unavailable as an experience because experience itself is determinate. Ax 23 does not resolve this tension. It installs the tension within philosophical thought.

XIV. The Transition from Ax 23 to Ax 24

Ax 23 is followed by “The End of Thought.” The transition can be read as structurally continuous rather than logically compulsory. Once thought confronts nothingness as the absence of all determination, it also confronts the limit of its own expressive operations.

Ax 24.1 — The limit of saying is not absence, but excess. [ (■ “Expression”) → (■ “Overflow”) → # ]

This proposition transforms the meaning of linguistic failure. Language does not necessarily stop because there is nothing to express. It may stop because what thought encounters cannot be contained within a determinate expression.

Nothingness is defined as absence of determination, yet the attempt to think it produces an excess of conceptual tension. Thought generates distinctions, negations, paradoxes, and recursive reflections. The limit of saying is reached not through conceptual emptiness but through the proliferation of attempts that cannot achieve identity with what they seek to express.

Ax 24.2 — Silence is the recursive pause through which thought reorganizes itself [ (■“Unsayable”) ↻ (■“Silence”) ⊑ (■“Reconfiguration”) ]

Silence is not identified with nothingness. Silence is a manifestation within thought’s recursive process. It is a pause, and therefore it possesses temporal and functional determination. Thought pauses and reorganizes itself. Silence is not nothingness; it is a determinate relation to the limit of expression.

Ax 24.3 — Philosophy is the boundary between what can be said and what can only be lived. [ (■ “Reflection”) ⊑ (■ “Existence”) → (■ “Direct Experience”) ]

Ax 23 brings thought to this boundary. Being manifests and can enter factual discourse. Nothingness cannot manifest as a fact without contradiction. Philosophy occupies the boundary where thought seeks to articulate this difference while recognizing that articulation has limits.

The movement from Ax 23 to Ax 24 is therefore a movement from ontological polarity to expressive limit. Thought begins with being and nothingness and arrives at the boundary between language and direct experience.

XV. Nothingness as an Operational Limit

Ax 23 should not be converted into a doctrine asserting the independent existence of nothingness as a hidden entity. Such a conversion would contradict Ax 23.1 by giving nothingness positive determination.

Within this reading, the concept of nothingness functions as an operational limit of thought. It allows thought to test the boundaries of manifestation, language, fact, possibility, and distinction.

BEINGBeing is clarified through manifestation in the world.
FACTA fact requires determination and relation.
POSSIBILITYA possibility remains a possible determination.
LANGUAGELanguage gives symbolic form to what is defined as without determination.
THOUGHTThought is itself a manifestation situated within the polarity.

The limit is productive because it prevents conceptual categories from collapsing into one another. Nothingness is not falsehood, illusion, absurdity, contingency, silence, the unknown, or unactualized possibility. Each of those has a determinate place within the architecture. Nothingness names the absence of the determination required for such placement.

XVI. The Central Tension of Ax 23

The central tension of Ax 23 can be stated as follows: philosophical thought must refer to nothingness, but every act of reference determines a sign or concept within thought.

Ax 23.3 does not solve this tension by claiming that thought possesses nothingness. The operator ⊑ indicates inclusion or derivation without exhaustion. Thought is included within the field configured by the two poles. It moves between them without converting either into a final possession.

Being gives thought content through manifestation. The concept of nothingness prevents the absence of all determination from being stabilized as a completed object of thought. Thought therefore oscillates between the impulse to articulate and the recognition that articulation cannot become total.

This movement protects philosophical thought from two opposite reductions. The first reduction is the claim that only manifested facts matter and that the limits of manifestation are meaningless. The second is the claim that nothingness invalidates all manifestation.

The pole of nothingness does not invalidate being. Being does not absorb the pole of nothingness into a fact. Their polarity remains active within thought.

XVII. Philosophical Thought as Sustained Polarity

Ax 23.3 presents philosophical thought as the capacity to remain within this polarity without prematurely dissolving it.

Ordinary thought often seeks resolution. It wants to decide whether something is or is not, whether a proposition is true or false, whether a fact is present or absent. These distinctions are necessary within determinate inquiry.

Philosophical thought, however, also investigates the conditions that make such distinctions possible. It asks what is meant by being, how manifestation becomes fact, how absence is represented, and where determination reaches its limit.

To remain between being and nothingness is not indecision. It is an active refusal to confuse a pole with its representation.

Thought must acknowledge being through the facts that manifest. It must also acknowledge that no configuration of facts can represent the absence of all determination. This dual acknowledgement prevents both naïve realism and empty abstraction.

The world unfolds as a relational set of facts, but thought can still ask what distinguishes manifestation from non-manifestation. Language traces a diagram of reality, but thought can still ask where the diagram reaches its limit. Action modifies reality, but thought can still ask why this configuration exists rather than another. Ax 23 gives these questions a common structure.

XVIII. Conclusion: At the Boundary of Manifestation

Ax 23 establishes nothingness and being as the poles of philosophical thought by distinguishing two radically different relations to determination.

Nothingness is the absence of all determination. It cannot be reduced to a determinate object described as empty, an unknown fact, an error, an absurd configuration, an unrealized possibility, or silence. Each of these remains determinate.

Being is what manifests in the world. Its manifestation becomes fact, and facts enter relational configurations. Being is therefore approached not as an isolated abstraction but through the concrete forms in which it appears, acts, transforms, and leaves traces.

Thought is situated between these poles. As a manifestation, thought belongs to being. As philosophical inquiry, it can orient itself toward the absence of determination. Yet it cannot transform nothingness into an ordinary object without contradicting what nothingness means.

The significance of Ax 23 lies in sustaining this impossibility as a productive field. The aim is not to resolve being into nothingness or nothingness into being. The aim is to clarify the tension through which manifestation becomes intelligible.

The thought of nothingness reveals that every determination has a limit. Factual manifestation requires thought to return from that limit to the world of relations. Philosophical thought therefore moves recursively between what appears and what cannot appear, between fact and the absence of factual determination, between language and its boundary.

Being manifests. Nothingness cannot be positively determined. Thought persists between them.
